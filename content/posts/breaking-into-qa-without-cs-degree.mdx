One of the most common questions I get is whether you need a computer
science degree to become a software tester. Short answer: no. Here's what
actually mattered.

## What got me in the door

- **A portfolio of real test artifacts**, not certificates. A written test
  plan for a small open-source project, a short Playwright suite against a
  public demo site, and a couple of clearly-written bug reports did more
  for my first interview than any resume line.
- **Clear written communication.** A huge, underrated part of testing is
  writing bug reports a developer can act on without asking five follow-up
  questions: steps to reproduce, expected vs. actual behavior, environment
  details.
- **Curiosity about how things break.** This is trainable, but it helps to
  already have the instinct to poke at edge cases rather than only follow
  the happy path.

## What I had to learn on the job

- **Just enough programming** to read and eventually write automated
  tests — not a full CS curriculum, but real comfort with variables,
  loops, functions, and reading a stack trace.
- **How the specific system under test actually works** — the business
  domain, the architecture, where the risky areas are. This is true
  regardless of your background; it just takes time on any new team.

## If you're starting from zero

A realistic first-90-days plan:

1. Learn manual, exploratory testing fundamentals on a real (even personal)
   project. Write down what you find.
2. Pick one automation tool (Playwright is a reasonable default in 2026)
   and write tests against a public site or your own small app.
3. Build 2–3 concrete artifacts: a test plan, an automated suite, and a
   handful of well-written bug reports.
4. Apply to QA/SDET-labeled roles specifically — the title varies a lot
   by company, so read the actual responsibilities, not just the title.

## Takeaway

A CS degree can help, but it's neither necessary nor sufficient. What
consistently mattered in my own path — and in every hiring conversation
I've since been part of — was demonstrable evidence you can find real
problems and communicate them clearly.
